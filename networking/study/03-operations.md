## Organizational Processes & Documentation

### Network Documentation & Diagrams

Accurate documentation is a network's ground truth, and it splits into distinct diagram types that answer different questions rather than one master document trying to do everything. A physical network diagram shows the actual hardware and how it is cabled together: which switch port connects to which patch panel jack, which uplink runs between two IDFs, exactly how a technician standing in a wiring closet would need to trace it. A logical network diagram instead abstracts that physical wiring away and shows how traffic actually flows: IP addressing, VLAN boundaries, and the Layer 3 relationships between devices, regardless of which literal cable happens to carry that traffic. Wiring diagrams and rack diagrams zoom in further, documenting individual cable runs and the exact rack-unit placement of each device. There is no single IEEE or IETF standard defining these diagram types or the symbols used to draw them — they are conventions built up across decades of networking practice and taught consistently across vendor and vendor-neutral curricula alike — but keeping them current is what turns an unplanned outage into a fast, targeted fix instead of a guessing game across unfamiliar infrastructure.

### Asset Inventory & IP Address Management (IPAM)

An asset inventory is the definitive list of every device connected to a network — make, model, serial number, physical location, and owner — and keeping it current is foundational enough that NIST SP 800-53's security and privacy control catalog places it within its own Configuration Management control family: an organization cannot secure, patch, or even locate a device it doesn't know it has. IP Address Management (IPAM) applies that same inventory discipline specifically to address space: it tracks which IP addresses are assigned, to which device, in which subnet, and whether that assignment came from a DHCP lease (RFC 2131) or a static configuration, while cross-referencing those addresses against the DNS hostnames (RFC 1035) that resolve to them. Done well, IPAM heads off the two failure modes that plague unmanaged address space — a duplicate address handed out twice, and exhaustion nobody saw coming because no one was tracking how much of a subnet was already spoken for. Like network diagramming, IPAM as a named discipline has no single RFC or ISO standard defining it; it is standard operational vocabulary layered on top of the DHCP and DNS protocols it manages, in the same spirit that NIST SP 800-53 treats maintaining an accurate system inventory as a baseline security control rather than an optional nicety.

### Service Level Agreements (SLAs) & Related Agreements

A Service Level Agreement (SLA) is a contract, typically between an organization and an external provider such as an ISP or a cloud vendor, that commits to specific, measurable service targets — uptime percentage, maximum response time to a reported outage, guaranteed bandwidth — along with the remedies, like service credits, that apply if the provider falls short. Uptime is usually expressed as a percentage of "nines": 99.9% ("three nines") allows for roughly 8.76 hours of downtime a year, while 99.999% ("five nines") allows for only about five minutes, and each additional nine costs the provider significantly more to guarantee. SLAs sit alongside a handful of related agreement types that cover different scope: a Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) documents a less formal, often non-binding mutual intent between two parties, while a Master Service Agreement (MSA) establishes the general terms governing an ongoing relationship, with individual projects then spelled out separately in a Statement of Work (SOW). None of these agreement types are protocol standards — they are business and legal vocabulary a network professional still needs to recognize, because SLA terms directly shape technical decisions like which circuit redundancy or monitoring thresholds are actually required to stay in compliance.

### Configuration Management (NIST SP 800-128)

Configuration management is the discipline of tracking, controlling, and documenting a system's settings so its actual, running state can always be compared against an approved baseline. NIST SP 800-128 frames this specifically as security-focused configuration management (SecCM): monitoring a system's configuration closely enough to keep organizational risk in check without getting in the way of whatever business function the system is actually there to provide, and it builds directly on top of the broader control catalog NIST SP 800-53 already establishes rather than replacing it. In network terms, this means every switch, router, and firewall configuration is version-controlled and compared against a known-good baseline, so a technician — or an automated tool — can immediately spot unauthorized drift, like a firewall rule quietly added outside any approved change, instead of discovering it only after something breaks or an attacker exploits it. Configuration management and change management (next) work together in practice: change management controls how a new configuration gets approved and deployed, while configuration management is what actually tracks, documents, and verifies the resulting state once that change lands.

### Change Management

Change management is the formal process an organization follows before modifying anything in production — a new firewall rule, a routing change, a switch firmware upgrade — so changes happen in a controlled, reviewable way instead of ad hoc. A typical process requires a documented change request describing what will change and why, a risk and impact assessment, review and approval by a change advisory board (CAB) or similar authority, a scheduled maintenance window to limit disruption to users, and — critically — a documented rollback plan in case the change doesn't go as expected. Emergency changes, like fixing an active outage, typically get an expedited approval path that still requires after-the-fact documentation, since skipping the process entirely is exactly how an unreviewed change becomes the next outage instead of the fix for one. Change management is standard IT operations vocabulary rather than a single citable protocol or spec, but it is the procedural discipline configuration management depends on: change management decides whether and how a modification is allowed to happen, while configuration management is what records and verifies the state that results.

### IT Asset Life-Cycle Management

Every piece of network hardware moves through a predictable life cycle: procurement (selecting and purchasing equipment that meets a documented need), deployment (installation, initial configuration, and addition to the asset inventory above), ongoing maintenance (patching, monitoring, periodic configuration review), and eventual retirement, reached in two milestones: end-of-life (EOL), when the manufacturer stops selling or enhancing the product (sometimes called end-of-sale) though limited support may continue, and the later end-of-support (EOS), when support and security patches stop entirely. Tracking where each asset sits in that life cycle matters operationally as much as financially: hardware running past its end-of-support date can no longer receive patches for newly discovered vulnerabilities, which quietly turns still-working-fine hardware into a growing security liability that configuration management alone can't fix. Decommissioning closes the loop — removing an asset from the network and the inventory, and securely wiping any data or credentials it held, so a retired device doesn't leave orphaned VLAN assignments, stale DNS records, or accessible leftover data behind. Like the other organizational processes in this section, asset life-cycle management is standard IT operations vocabulary rather than a single citable spec, but it depends directly on the asset inventory and configuration management practices covered earlier here to actually track where each device stands.

## Monitoring, Logging & Discovery

### SNMP: Versions and the Real v2c-to-v3 Security Gap

SNMP (Simple Network Management Protocol) lets a central management station query devices for status and configuration information, and its architecture — defined in RFC 3411 — evolved through three versions that get routinely confused on exactly the point that matters most: security. SNMPv1 and SNMPv2c both authenticate using nothing more than a community string, a shared cleartext value sent with every request; RFC 3411 itself describes this community-based approach as providing only rudimentary authentication and access control, not real security — it isn't encrypted, and it functions closer to an unlocked shared password than genuine access control. The real improvement SNMPv2c added over SNMPv1 was functionality, not security: most notably the GetBulkRequest operation, which lets a manager retrieve a large batch of values in a single round trip instead of one Get at a time — a meaningfully faster query on a device with a large MIB, but not a more secure one. Genuine authentication and encryption arrived only with SNMPv3, built around what RFC 3411 names the User-based Security Model (USM): it verifies who is actually making a request and can encrypt SNMP traffic in transit, closing what the same RFC calls out as the most significant security gap left open by SNMPv1 and SNMPv2c. A common wrong answer credits SNMPv2 itself with adding encryption; it did not — real security had to wait for v3's USM.

### Flow Data & NetFlow (RFC 7011 / IPFIX)

Flow-based monitoring takes a fundamentally different approach from full packet capture: instead of storing entire packet contents, a flow-capable router or switch records metadata about each conversation — source and destination address, ports, protocol, byte and packet counts, timing — and exports that summary to a collector for analysis. RFC 7011 formally specifies IPFIX (IP Flow Information Export), the IETF standard for this kind of export, and defines a flow as the group of packets crossing a given observation point during some interval that share a common set of properties, such as the same source and destination address pair and port numbers. IPFIX itself grew directly out of Cisco's proprietary NetFlow — its message-header version number simply increments the value used by NetFlow's own export format — which is why NetFlow and IPFIX get mentioned in the same breath even though IPFIX is the vendor-neutral, standardized descendant most modern equipment actually implements. Because flow records carry only metadata rather than payload, they are dramatically smaller to store and faster to query than full packet captures, making flow monitoring the practical choice for continuously profiling traffic patterns and bandwidth consumption across an entire network — leaving full packet capture, covered next, for the smaller number of investigations that actually need to see inside a conversation's contents.

### Packet Capture with tcpdump

Packet capture tools record full packet contents rather than just flow metadata, which is what makes them the right tool for actually inspecting what is happening inside a specific conversation instead of just measuring how much traffic it generated. tcpdump is the standard command-line packet analyzer on Linux and Unix-like systems: it prints, or with the -w flag saves to a file for later analysis, a description of every packet on a network interface that matches a filter expression, timestamped by default down to a fraction of a second past midnight. Capturing meaningful traffic on a switched network usually requires putting the capturing interface into promiscuous mode, so the NIC accepts and hands up every frame it sees rather than only frames addressed to it — the same underlying limitation that makes port mirroring (below) necessary in the first place, since a switch normally only forwards a frame out the one port where its destination actually lives. Saved captures are commonly analyzed afterward in a full graphical tool like Wireshark, which layers protocol decoding and filtering on top of the same underlying packet data tcpdump captures. Because a full capture stores every byte of every matched packet, it does not scale the way flow monitoring does, which is exactly why the two are complementary rather than substitutes for each other: flow data for continuous, network-wide visibility, packet capture for a focused, evidence-level look at one specific problem.

### Log Aggregation & SIEM

Individual devices — routers, switches, firewalls, servers — all generate their own local logs, but a log sitting only on the device that created it is of limited use during an investigation: it can be lost if the device is compromised or reset, and there is no easy way to correlate one device's entries against another's without pulling each source separately. Log aggregation solves that by forwarding logs from every device to one centralized collection point in near-real time, commonly over the Syslog protocol, so nothing depends on any single device staying reachable or intact. A SIEM (Security Information and Event Management) platform builds on top of that centralized log stream: beyond just storing logs, it correlates events across many different sources, applies rules or analytics to flag patterns that look suspicious — a failed login on one system followed immediately by a successful login somewhere else, for example — and gives an analyst a single console to search and investigate across the whole environment instead of one device at a time. Neither log aggregation nor SIEM is defined by a specific RFC the way SNMP or IPFIX are; they are standard operational and security vocabulary describing a practice and a category of product, built on top of whatever underlying protocols the devices themselves already use to generate the data being aggregated.

### Port Mirroring: SPAN & RSPAN

A switch normally forwards each frame only out the one port where its destination lives, which is efficient but creates a problem for any tool — an IDS, a packet analyzer, a SIEM's log collector — that needs to see a copy of traffic it isn't actually addressed to. Port mirroring solves this by configuring a switch to copy traffic from one or more source ports, or an entire VLAN, out to a separate destination port, where a monitoring device can plug in and passively observe without ever sitting inline in the actual traffic path. SPAN (Switched Port Analyzer) is the common name for this feature when the source and destination ports live on the same physical switch; RSPAN (Remote SPAN) extends the same idea across multiple switches by carrying the mirrored traffic over a dedicated VLAN to a destination port on a different switch elsewhere in the network. This is the same mechanism that lets an IDS see a full copy of live traffic without being able to block or alter it — passive, out-of-band visibility, which is exactly why an IDS is limited to alerting rather than actively intervening the way an inline IPS can. SPAN and RSPAN are Cisco-originated terms without an IETF or IEEE standard behind them, but the underlying technique is implemented, often under different names, on switches from virtually every vendor.

### Network Discovery & Scanning (Nmap)

Network discovery is the process of actively finding out what is actually connected to a network and how it is configured, rather than relying only on documentation that may already be stale. Nmap ("Network Mapper") is the standard open-source tool for this: it sends crafted IP packets to a target range and uses the responses, or the lack of them, to determine which hosts are up, which ports and services are open on each one, what application and version is running behind each open port, and — through OS-fingerprinting techniques that examine subtle differences in how various operating systems' TCP/IP stacks respond — what operating system a host is likely running. A basic host-discovery scan answers only whether something is present at an address, while a full port and service scan goes further and profiles exactly what each live host is running and how it might be reachable. Discovery tools like this cut both ways: the same scan an administrator runs to keep documentation and the asset inventory accurate is functionally identical to reconnaissance an attacker would run against the same network, which is why unexpected or unauthorized scanning activity showing up in flow data or logs is itself a meaningful security signal worth investigating.

## Business Continuity & High Availability

### Maximum Tolerable Downtime & Recovery Time Objective (MTD, RTO)

NIST SP 800-34 Rev. 1, the federal Contingency Planning Guide, defines three related recovery metrics that get tested precisely because they sound similar but answer different questions. Maximum Tolerable Downtime (MTD) is the broadest of the three: the total amount of time a system owner is willing to accept for a given mission or business process to be down, folding in every downstream impact of that outage. Recovery Time Objective (RTO) narrows that down to one specific system resource: the maximum amount of time that resource can stay unavailable before it causes an unacceptable impact on other resources and on the business process MTD was defined around — in the guide's own framing, RTO is a subset consideration feeding into the broader MTD, not a separate, unrelated figure. Because RTO exists specifically to keep MTD from being exceeded, RTO must normally come in shorter than MTD: choosing recovery technology capable of meeting a tight RTO is exactly how an organization keeps a fast-moving outage from ballooning into a violation of the maximum downtime the business can actually tolerate. Both numbers are forward-looking — they describe how long recovery is allowed to take, not what happens to the data itself during that recovery, which is where RPO, covered next, comes in.

### Recovery Point Objective (RPO): RTO vs. RPO

Recovery Point Objective (RPO) is easy to confuse with RTO because both are core NIST SP 800-34 Rev. 1 recovery metrics, but they measure genuinely different things and point in opposite directions. RTO is forward-looking: it caps how long a resource is allowed to stay down, counting forward from the moment of failure. RPO is backward-looking instead: it identifies the point in time, before the disruption happened, to which the business process's data must be recoverable — in practice, set by how recent the last usable backup actually was. The guide is explicit that RPO is not folded into MTD the way RTO is; it instead answers a separate question entirely — how much data loss the business process can tolerate during recovery — independent of how long the outage itself lasts. The practical consequence is that an organization can meet its RTO, getting a system back online quickly, while still failing its RPO if the backup it restored from was hours or days stale; conversely, a very tight RPO from near-continuous replication does nothing to guarantee a fast RTO if the failover process itself is slow. Getting the direction right matters here specifically: RTO is about acceptable downtime duration, RPO is about acceptable data loss measured backward to the last good backup.

### DR Site Types: Cold, Warm & Hot Sites

When a primary facility becomes unusable, an organization needs an alternate site to resume operations, and NIST SP 800-34 Rev. 1 categorizes the common options by how ready each one is to take over. A cold site provides only basic space and infrastructure — power, telecommunications connectivity, environmental controls — with no pre-installed system hardware, making it the cheapest option to maintain but the slowest to actually bring a system back online, since equipment still has to be acquired and installed after the disaster happens. A warm site is partially equipped: some or all of the system hardware, software, and telecommunications gear is already in place, positioning it in the middle of both the cost and readiness spectrum. A hot site is fully configured and appropriately sized to support the system's requirements immediately, with the necessary hardware, infrastructure, and support personnel already in place — the most expensive of the three to maintain, but capable of the fastest recovery, closest to zero downtime. Variations exist beyond these three baseline types — a mobile site is a self-contained, transportable facility that can be delivered where it's needed, and a mirrored site is a fully redundant, real-time replica of the primary site — but cold, warm, and hot remain the three reference points nearly every DR conversation is framed around.

### High Availability Approaches: Redundancy & Failover

High availability (HA) is the broader design goal that cold/warm/hot DR sites serve one large-scale version of: keeping a system reachable and functioning through the failure of any single component, without waiting for a full-blown disaster. The core technique underneath nearly every HA design is redundancy — deploying more than one instance of a critical component so a single failure doesn't take the whole system down — paired with failover, the mechanism that actually detects a failure and shifts traffic or workload onto the surviving redundant component. That pattern shows up throughout networking at every layer: a First Hop Redundancy Protocol like HSRP or VRRP keeps a default gateway reachable if one router fails, link aggregation keeps a connection up if one physical link in a bundle fails, and Spanning Tree keeps a redundant switch topology loop-free while still holding backup paths in reserve. HA deployments are commonly described as active-active, where every redundant component handles live traffic simultaneously and only some capacity is lost if one fails, or active-passive, where a standby component sits idle until a failure promotes it into service — a distinction that maps directly onto the active/standby roles a protocol like HSRP already uses for its own gateway redundancy.

### DR Testing: Tabletop vs. Functional Exercises

A disaster recovery plan that has never been tested is really just a hypothesis, so NIST SP 800-34 Rev. 1 calls for regularly exercising the plan rather than leaving it to sit unopened until an actual emergency. A tabletop exercise is discussion-based only: personnel meet in a classroom-style setting, a facilitator walks the group through a scenario, and participants talk through their roles, responsibilities, and decisions without deploying any actual equipment or systems — appropriate, per the guide, as the baseline testing cadence for lower-impact systems. A functional exercise goes further by actually validating operational readiness in a simulated environment: staff execute their real roles and procedures — communications, emergency notifications, system equipment setup — as they would during a genuine incident, just against a simulated rather than a real disruption, scaling up to a full-scale functional exercise that includes an actual system failover for the highest-impact systems. The jump in rigor between the two matters: a tabletop exercise can reveal that a plan's roles and decision points are unclear, but only a functional exercise can reveal that the failover procedure itself doesn't actually work as documented, which is exactly the kind of gap a discussion alone can never surface.

### MTBF vs. MTTR: Reliability Engineering Metrics

MTBF (Mean Time Between Failures) and MTTR (Mean Time To Repair, sometimes Mean Time To Recovery) are reliability-engineering metrics that get grouped with RTO/RPO/MTD because they all describe uptime and recovery, but they come from a different lineage — general reliability engineering rather than NIST SP 800-34's contingency-planning framework — and they measure a device's or system's own failure behavior rather than a business-chosen downtime target. MTBF is a predictive average: the expected time a piece of repairable hardware or a system will run between one failure and the next, typically derived from historical failure data or a manufacturer's own testing. MTTR is the average time it actually takes to detect a failure, diagnose it, and restore the failed component or system to working order once a failure has already happened. The two together sketch a simple reliability picture: a high MTBF means failures are rare, while a low MTTR means that when a failure does happen, the system is back up quickly again — and unlike RTO and RPO, which are business-defined targets an organization chooses, MTBF and MTTR are largely measured, empirical properties of the equipment itself.

## IP Services & Secure Access Methods

### DHCP: Lease Process, Relay & SLAAC

DHCP (RFC 2131) assigns an IPv4 address to a joining host through a four-message exchange commonly abbreviated DORA: the client broadcasts a DHCPDISCOVER looking for any server, servers reply with a DHCPOFFER proposing an address, the client broadcasts a DHCPREQUEST accepting one specific offer so any other servers know theirs wasn't taken, and the chosen server confirms with a DHCPACK. Because a DHCPDISCOVER is only a local broadcast, a DHCP server living on a different subnet from the client needs a BOOTP/DHCP relay agent — commonly a router configured with an "ip helper"-style command — to forward the broadcast on as a unicast, stamping in the relay agent's own address so the server knows which subnet to allocate an address from. Every lease also carries two renewal timers: T1, defaulting to 50% of the lease duration, is when the client starts trying to renew with its original server, and T2, defaulting to 87.5% of the lease, is when it broadens that attempt to any server in case the original one is unreachable. IPv6 offers a path that skips DHCP entirely: SLAAC (Stateless Address Autoconfiguration, RFC 4862) lets a host generate its own global address from the network prefix a router advertises, verifying the result is actually unique on the link through Duplicate Address Detection before using it — a stateless approach that DHCPv6 can still complement when an administrator needs to hand out settings, like DNS servers, that SLAAC alone doesn't cover.

### DNS Record Types & Zone Types

A DNS zone is the administrative unit a name server is authoritative for — the actual data for a domain, held in a master file (RFC 1035) on a primary server, with one or more secondary servers periodically pulling updates from that primary through a zone transfer to stay in sync as an authoritative backup. Within a zone, different resource record types answer different kinds of questions: an A record maps a hostname to an IPv4 address, with AAAA doing the same job for IPv6 once that record type was added as DNS grew to support the newer address family; a CNAME record is an alias pointing one hostname at another's canonical name; an MX record identifies which server handles mail for a domain; a TXT record holds arbitrary descriptive text, commonly used today for domain-ownership verification and email-security policies; and a PTR record does the reverse of an A record, mapping an IP address back to a hostname. Every zone also has exactly one SOA (Start of Authority) record, which RFC 1035 defines as marking the start of a zone of authority and carrying the zone's own administrative parameters, like the refresh interval secondary servers use when checking the primary for updates. Zones also split along a second axis, forward zones (name-to-address) versus reverse zones (address-to-name, built from PTR records), which is why a fully documented DNS deployment maintains both directions rather than just the forward lookups end users actually type into a browser.

### DNSSEC (RFC 4033)

Plain DNS has no way to prove that a response actually came from a zone's real authoritative source and wasn't tampered with in transit — a gap that made cache-poisoning and off-path spoofing attacks possible for decades. DNSSEC (DNS Security Extensions), introduced in RFC 4033, closes that gap by adding data origin authentication and data integrity to DNS responses, without touching DNS's basic query-and-response mechanics or attempting to provide confidentiality — a DNSSEC-signed answer is still sent in the clear, just cryptographically verifiable. It works by having each zone sign its own records with a private key, publishing the corresponding public key in a DNSKEY record, and chaining trust upward: a resolver that already trusts a parent zone's key can verify a DS (Delegation Signer) record pointing to the child zone's key, and follow that chain link by link up to a trust anchor it was configured with directly, rather than trusting every layer blindly. A resolver performing this validation is described as security-aware; one that isn't simply ignores the extra signature records and behaves exactly like classic DNS. Because DNSSEC only adds authentication and integrity, it does nothing to encrypt the contents of a DNS query itself — that gap in DNS privacy is what DoH and DoT, covered next, were built to close instead.

### Encrypted DNS: DoH vs. DoT

DoH (DNS over HTTPS, RFC 8484) and DoT (DNS over TLS, RFC 7858) both encrypt DNS queries so they can't be read or tampered with in transit, but they make deliberately opposite choices about where that traffic lives on the network, and getting the direction right matters. DoT runs DNS over a dedicated, well-known TCP port — port 853 — reserved specifically for encrypted DNS traffic; RFC 7858 explicitly prohibits sending cleartext DNS over that port, which makes DoT traffic easy for a network to identify and separately filter, permit, or block by port number alone. DoH takes the opposite approach on purpose: it carries DNS queries inside ordinary HTTPS on the standard web port 443, wrapping each query and response as an HTTP exchange rather than giving DNS a dedicated port of its own. RFC 8484 states that reusing the HTTPS default port, and mixing DoH traffic in with other ordinary HTTPS traffic on the same connection, is what deters on-path devices from interfering with DNS resolution — meaning DoH traffic is deliberately indistinguishable from regular web browsing at the transport layer, a privacy feature rather than an accident of design. That same property cuts both ways operationally: it is exactly why DoT is easier for an administrator to selectively manage by port, while DoH is much harder to control or block without also disrupting unrelated HTTPS traffic on port 443.

### Time Synchronization: NTP & PTP

Accurate, synchronized clocks matter more in networking than they might seem — log timestamps only let an analyst correlate events across devices if every device's clock agrees, and some authentication protocols and certificate validations fail outright if a device's clock drifts too far from reality. NTP (Network Time Protocol, RFC 5905) is the standard mechanism for this over ordinary IP networks: clients synchronize against a hierarchy of time servers, and NTPv4's mitigation and discipline algorithms can hold accuracy to the tens of microseconds on a modern workstation over a fast LAN — more than precise enough for log correlation, authentication windows, and everyday scheduling. Some applications need tighter precision than that: industrial control systems, financial trading platforms, and telecom infrastructure often require sub-microsecond synchronization across an entire network, which is what PTP (Precision Time Protocol, IEEE 1588) is built for. IEEE 1588 itself is a paywalled standard with no free canonical text, but per a Cisco configuration guide describing a practical PTP deployment, the protocol elects a grandmaster clock through a Best Master Clock Algorithm that continuously reassesses which clock on the network is the best available reference, and a well-configured PTP network can hold slave devices synchronized to within about 1 microsecond of the grandmaster even 16 hops away — an order of magnitude tighter than NTP's typical accuracy, at the cost of needing PTP-aware switches and hardware throughout the path rather than running over any ordinary IP network the way NTP does.

### VPN Types, Remote Access & the Jump Box

VPNs come in two structural types depending on what's being connected: a site-to-site VPN builds a permanent, always-on encrypted tunnel between two networks — commonly two office locations — so devices on each side can reach the other transparently without running their own VPN client, while a client-to-site (remote-access) VPN instead connects one individual user's device into the network on demand, authenticating that user each time a connection is established. Remote-access VPNs are further split by how much of a client's traffic they carry: a full-tunnel configuration routes all of that client's traffic through the VPN, including ordinary internet browsing, giving the organization visibility over everything at the cost of overhead, while a split-tunnel configuration routes only traffic destined for the private network through the tunnel and lets everything else leave directly over the user's own internet connection. For securing administrative access specifically, a jump box (jump server) acts as a single, tightly monitored gateway that administrators must pass through before reaching any sensitive internal system, so direct administrative access from an ordinary workstation is never allowed. That same idea underlies in-band versus out-of-band management: in-band management reaches a device over the same production network it normally forwards traffic on, while out-of-band management reaches it over a separate path — a dedicated management VLAN, or a console port wired to a separate console server — so a device can still be reached and recovered even when the production network itself is what's down.
