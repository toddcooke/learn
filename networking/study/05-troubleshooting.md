## Troubleshooting Methodology

### Why Structured Troubleshooting Beats Guesswork

Modern networks have grown too complex for random trial and error to be a reliable fix strategy: multiple media types, multiple protocols running side by side, and links reaching into networks an organization doesn't itself administer all combine to make the real source of a problem hard to pin down quickly. Cisco's own internetworking troubleshooting guide makes the case for structure directly, warning that skipping a systematic method burns through time and resources and can leave a network worse off than before anyone started poking at it. The fix isn't a specific tool or command — it's a repeatable process: define what's actually failing, narrow the field of possible causes using real evidence rather than a hunch, and change one thing at a time so whichever fix works can be identified and repeated the next time the same symptom shows up. That same discipline is exactly what the CompTIA seven-step process, covered next, is built to enforce on the exam.

### Cisco's General Problem-Solving Model

Beyond urging a systematic mindset, Cisco's guide lays out a concrete sequence for getting there: write a precise problem statement covering both the symptoms observed and the candidate causes that could produce them; collect supporting evidence from affected users, other administrators, management systems, protocol-analyzer traces, and diagnostic command output; use that evidence to eliminate candidates and narrow the list down to the most plausible one remaining; build a plan around that single leading candidate, adjusting just one variable per attempt so the effect of each change stays traceable; carry the plan out step by step while watching for the symptom to clear; gather fresh results the same way the original evidence was collected; and judge whether the fix actually worked. If it didn't, the model loops back to the next most likely candidate rather than starting over from scratch, repeating the cycle until the real cause is found. It's a strong structural match for the exam's own seven-step process, even though the exact stage names differ.

### The CompTIA Seven-Step Process

CompTIA's own exam objectives describe network troubleshooting as a seven-stage cycle, and while the precise wording of each stage is CompTIA's own copyrighted phrasing rather than something drawn from an external standard, the stage names themselves function as standard exam vocabulary worth knowing in order: identify the problem, establish a theory of probable cause, test the theory, establish a plan of action, implement the solution, verify full system functionality, and document findings. In practice the flow mirrors what Cisco's own model already teaches — gather enough detail from the reported symptoms and any affected users to state the problem precisely, form a working hypothesis about what's actually causing it, then check that hypothesis against real evidence before committing to a fix. Once a fix is planned, carried out, and confirmed to have restored normal operation everywhere it needs to, the final stage is what turns a one-off repair into something the next technician can benefit from: a written record of what broke, why, and how it got resolved.

### Top-Down, Bottom-Up & Divide-and-Conquer Approaches

Once a theory needs testing, a technician still has to decide where in the stack to actually start looking, and three approaches cover most of the options taught alongside CompTIA's process. A top-down approach starts at the application layer and works downward — sensible when a symptom looks application-specific, like one broken web request, since it rules out purely upper-layer explanations first. A bottom-up approach starts at the physical layer instead and works upward, which fits a symptom that smells like cabling, a bad port, or no link light at all. Divide-and-conquer skips both extremes and starts in the middle, typically the network layer, checking connectivity there first and letting the result point up or down from that midpoint — a reasonable default when there's no strong hint yet about which end of the stack is actually broken. These three approaches don't appear in Cisco's own troubleshooting guide cited above, which describes a single general problem-solving model instead — they're standard troubleshooting vocabulary taught throughout networking curricula, built directly on the OSI layer model covered earlier in this material, giving a practical use for a model that can otherwise feel abstract.

### Confirming a Theory Before Committing to a Fix

The middle stretch of both Cisco's model and the CompTIA process is where an inexperienced technician is most likely to go wrong: jumping straight from a first guess to a fix without ever actually confirming the guess was right. Cisco's model spells out the discipline this stage needs — build a plan around the single most likely candidate, but change only one variable when carrying it out, because touching several things at once might still clear the symptom while leaving no way to know which change actually mattered. If the fix doesn't work, the correct response isn't to keep piling more changes onto the same theory; it's to treat that theory as ruled out, fall back to the next most plausible candidate, and start the plan-implement-verify cycle again. Escalating to a more experienced technician or a vendor's support line is itself a legitimate outcome once a technician has exhausted the theories they can test and confirm on their own, rather than a failure of the process — the goal is landing on a verified cause, not just narrowing candidates forever.

### Documentation, Baselines & Staying Ready for the Next Failure

Good troubleshooting doesn't start when a symptom appears — Cisco's guide frames most of the real preparation as work done well before any failure, in the form of accurate, current documentation that support staff can actually rely on under pressure. That means keeping an up-to-date physical and logical map of the network, a list of every protocol in use and where it runs, current router and switch configurations, and a record of every external network connection along with the routing protocol used to reach it. Just as important is an established performance baseline: without a documented picture of what normal utilization, latency, and error rates look like at different times of day, there's no reliable way to tell whether today's numbers are genuinely abnormal or just an ordinary busy afternoon. The final stage of CompTIA's own seven-step process, documenting findings, feeds directly back into that same preparedness cycle — a written record of a solved problem becomes exactly the reference material a future technician needs to recognize and resolve the same symptom faster the next time it appears.

## Physical Layer & Cabling Problems

### Cable Categories & the Cat6 vs. Cat6a Distance Limit

Twisted-pair category ratings, introduced earlier in this material's structured-cabling coverage, matter most in troubleshooting when a design pushes right up against a category's real limits rather than its headline speed. Cat6 is rated for 10GBASE-T, but only over a shortened run — commonly cited in the roughly 37-to-55-meter range depending on how much alien crosstalk (interference bleeding in from other cables bundled nearby) the installation actually has to contend with — it was never certified to carry a full 10Gbps connection across the standard 100-meter horizontal run the way Cat5e and Cat6 both comfortably handle at 1Gbps. Cat6a closes that gap specifically by adding shielding that suppresses alien crosstalk, which is what lets it guarantee the full 10Gbps over the complete 100-meter distance rather than a shortened, condition-dependent one. The practical consequence shows up constantly on the exam and in real designs alike: a 90-meter run that needs to carry 10Gbps calls for Cat6a, not Cat6, even though Cat6 does support 10GBASE-T at shorter range — the deciding factor is the actual cable length measured against the category's guaranteed distance, not simply whether the category theoretically supports the target speed at all.

### Signal Degradation: Attenuation, Crosstalk & Interference

Three related but distinct problems account for most non-broken-cable signal quality issues on copper runs. Attenuation is simply signal strength fading as it travels down a cable — enough of it, and a receiver can no longer reliably tell a 1 from a 0, which is exactly why every cable category comes with a maximum supported distance in the first place. Crosstalk is interference from a cable's own neighbors: near-end crosstalk (NEXT) leaks between the twisted pairs inside the very same cable close to the transmitting end, while alien crosstalk leaks in from entirely separate cables bundled or run in parallel nearby, the specific problem Cat6a's added shielding exists to suppress. Electromagnetic interference (EMI) from an external, non-cabling source — fluorescent lighting ballasts, motors, or heavy electrical equipment sharing a conduit path — degrades a copper signal the same way, which is why cable runs get routed away from that kind of equipment whenever possible. Fiber-optic cabling, carrying data as light rather than electrical voltage, is immune to both crosstalk and EMI entirely, which is one of the reasons a fiber run replaces copper in electrically noisy environments or once a distance limit is reached that no copper category can solve.

### Improper Termination & Cable Faults

A cable that tests badly is very often a termination problem rather than a defect in the cable itself. The two wiring standards for terminating twisted-pair jacks and plugs, T568A and T568B, define slightly different pin-to-pair color mappings, and mixing the two on opposite ends of the same run — accidentally wiring one end to T568A and the other to T568B — produces a crossover pairing that will not function as a straight-through link, though it happens to work by coincidence for direct switch-free connections between two like devices. A split pair is a subtler termination mistake: the individual wires are technically continuous end to end, so a basic continuity check can pass, but two wires that should have stayed together as one twisted pair get separated onto two different pairs during termination, destroying that pair's ability to cancel out crosstalk and degrading the link even though every wire still technically connects. Opens (a wire that never makes contact at all) and shorts (two wires touching that shouldn't be) round out the most common physical faults, and all of them point to the same root cause: a termination job that wasn't done, or wasn't verified, carefully enough.

### Interface Counters: CRC Errors, Collisions & Duplex Mismatch

A switch's interface statistics separate error counters into categories that point toward genuinely different layers of problem, which is exactly why Cisco's own switch-port troubleshooting guidance calls out counters like CRC, frame, runts, and giants as distinct fields to check alongside collision counts rather than treating every error the same way. A CRC error means a frame arrived with contents that don't match its own checksum — the frame got corrupted somewhere in transit — and that kind of corruption is a physical-layer event: a bad cable segment, a failing connector, or a marginal transceiver, not something caused by two devices contending for the same shared medium. That distinction is what makes a rising CRC-error count paired with a collision count sitting at zero a meaningful diagnostic signal on its own: since frame corruption isn't a collision-domain event, that specific combination points straight at the physical layer and away from anything related to contention on the link. Collisions still matter on their own, though, and one specific kind carries its own diagnostic weight: a late collision — one detected only after a device has already sent a significant part of its frame — is standard Network+ vocabulary for the signature most closely tied to a duplex mismatch, exactly the scenario where one side of a link sends without doing carrier sense at all while the other side is still transmitting. Cisco's own STP troubleshooting guidance cited above describes that same underlying scenario — the sending bridge simply 'detects a collision' and backs off — without using the specific term 'late collision' itself, and that corruption of both sides' traffic eventually starves Spanning Tree of the BPDUs it needs, as covered later in this domain, to keep from forming a loop.

### PoE Power Budgets: 802.3af, 802.3at & 802.3bt

Power over Ethernet figures come in two flavors that are easy to conflate: what a switch port is rated to supply, and what the connected device is actually guaranteed to receive once cable resistance has taken its share. Cisco's own account of PoE's evolution confirms the headline port-side numbers directly: 802.3at (PoE+, Type 2), ratified in 2009, raised port power to 30W; Cisco's own 60W Universal PoE implementation fed directly into 802.3bt's 60W Type 3 tier; and that same single amendment, IEEE 802.3bt-2018, defined the roughly 90W Type 4 ceiling alongside Type 3 rather than in a separate later revision. IEEE 802.3af/at/bt are themselves paywalled standards with no free canonical text, so the finer-grained numbers below are treated as standard PoE figures cited consistently across networking curricula rather than traced to one citable source: 802.3af (Type 1) supplies up to 15.4W at the port but guarantees only 12.95W actually reaching the device after cable loss; 802.3at (Type 2) guarantees 25.5W at the device against its 30W port maximum; and 802.3bt's Type 3 and Type 4 guarantee roughly 51W and 71.3W at the device respectively, against port maximums of 60W and roughly 90-100W depending on the source (Cisco's own account above states 90W flat). That device-side number is the one that actually matters when a question describes a specific device's power draw: a device rated to need 25W of guaranteed power has to sit on at least an 802.3at port, since only 802.3at's 25.5W device-side guarantee — not its 30W port-side maximum — clears that requirement; reading the port's raw maximum instead of the guaranteed device-side figure is exactly the mistake that leads to a real device being underpowered.

### Transceivers: SFP/SFP+ Mismatches & Compatibility

Modern switches and routers commonly reach fiber or alternate copper media through a modular transceiver slot rather than a fixed port, and getting the transceiver wrong is its own common category of physical-layer failure separate from the cable itself. SFP (Small Form-factor Pluggable) transceivers commonly support up to 1Gbps, SFP+ extends the same physical form factor to 10Gbps, and QSFP (Quad SFP) bundles four channels together for 40Gbps and beyond — the same port hardware can often physically accept any of them, which is exactly the trap: a link negotiates at the speed of whichever transceiver is actually installed, so plugging a 1Gbps SFP into a port capable of 10Gbps silently caps that link at the transceiver's own lower speed rather than the port's real maximum. Transceivers are also wavelength- and mode-specific on the fiber side — single-mode and multimode fiber use different transceivers built for their respective light sources and cannot simply be swapped for one another — and pairing a single-mode transceiver on one end of a link with a multimode transceiver on the other typically leaves the link down entirely rather than merely degraded, since the two are built around fundamentally incompatible optics rather than just different speeds. As with cable testers and Wi-Fi analyzers covered later in this domain, SFP/SFP+/QSFP are standard hardware vocabulary rather than terms defined in any single document cited here.

## Network & Service-Layer Issues

### STP Loops & BPDU Failures

Spanning Tree loops, introduced earlier in this material's switching coverage as the problem STP exists to prevent, almost always trace back to the same underlying mechanism when they actually happen in practice: a blocked port transitions into forwarding because it stopped receiving the BPDUs that were telling it to stay blocked. Cisco's own STP troubleshooting guidance is direct about this — most reported spanning-tree failures aren't a software bug at all, they're a massive, sustained loss of BPDUs on a link that's still technically up, which leaves a switch with no way to tell that a better path to the root bridge is still supposed to be blocking somewhere else. Duplex mismatch, covered earlier in this domain, is one of the most common root causes: the same collisions and packet corruption it produces on ordinary data traffic hit the BPDUs traveling across that same link just as hard, and once a switch stops hearing from what it thought was the root bridge, it unblocks a port that should have stayed blocking and completes a loop. A unidirectional link failure — a connection that passes traffic in one direction but silently drops it in the other — produces the identical symptom through a different physical cause, which is why confirming BPDUs are actually still arriving on a suspect port, not just that the link light is on, is the real first move once an STP loop is suspected.

### VLAN Assignment Issues

A device landing on the wrong VLAN is one of the most common everyday service-layer complaints, and it almost always comes down to a mismatch between what a port is actually configured for and what the device plugged into it expects. An access port assigned to the wrong VLAN silently drops a device into a broadcast domain with the wrong subnet, gateway, and reachable resources — the device itself often shows no error at all, since from its own point of view it associated and pulled an address successfully, just from the wrong DHCP scope. A trunk port carrying the wrong set of allowed VLANs, or missing one entirely from its allowed list, produces a related but distinct symptom: traffic for that VLAN never crosses the trunk at all, so devices on either side of it can't reach each other even though each individual access port looks correctly configured on its own end. A native VLAN mismatch between the two ends of the same trunk, introduced earlier in this material's coverage of 802.1Q tagging, is its own special case worth remembering specifically because it doesn't just block traffic — traffic for the mismatched native VLAN on either end crosses the trunk untagged and lands in whichever VLAN the receiving switch treats as native, quietly leaking frames into a VLAN they were never meant to reach.

### ACL Misconfiguration

Access control lists, covered earlier in this material's security-domain coverage, fail in troubleshooting-relevant ways that tend to repeat across otherwise unrelated networks. Rule order is the most common trap: because an ACL evaluates top to bottom and stops at the first match, a broad rule placed too early can silently shadow a more specific rule sitting further down the list that was actually meant to take effect, so traffic gets permitted or denied by whichever rule happens to match first rather than whichever rule an administrator intended to be authoritative. The implicit deny at the end of every ACL causes its own class of trouble ticket: a newly added service that needs a specific permit rule simply won't work until that rule is added, and because the implicit deny generates no configuration line an administrator can see and second-guess, it's an easy step to forget entirely. Applying an ACL to the wrong interface, or in the wrong direction on the right interface — inbound versus outbound relative to the router evaluating it — produces a result that looks identical to a correctly written ACL simply not being applied at all, which is why confirming both the interface and the direction is one of the first things worth checking once an ACL is suspected of blocking something it shouldn't.

### Routing Table Issues

A routing problem shows up as connectivity that works in one direction and mysteriously fails in the other, or as a destination that's reachable from some parts of a network but not others, and the routing table itself is usually where the real cause becomes visible. A missing route is the simplest case: no entry exists for a destination network at all, and the router falls back to its default route if one exists or drops the traffic outright if it doesn't. A route that exists but points the wrong way — a stale static route left over from a since-changed topology, or a dynamic route that hasn't yet reconverged after a link failure — actively sends traffic toward a dead end rather than simply failing to find a path, which is often harder to spot than a route that's just missing. Administrative distance, covered earlier in this material's routing coverage, causes its own particular class of confusion: when two different routing sources both offer a path to the same destination, the router installs only the one with the lower administrative distance regardless of which path is actually shorter or faster, so a technician staring at a seemingly reasonable OSPF route that never makes it into the routing table needs to check whether a lower-AD static route to the same destination is quietly winning instead.

### DHCP Problems: Ports, DORA Failures & DHCPDECLINE/DHCPNAK

DHCP problems tend to cluster around a small number of repeat offenders, starting with the port numbers themselves: a DHCP server listens on UDP port 67 while clients receive responses on UDP port 68, and those two ports sit immediately next to UDP port 69, TFTP's port — a detail worth locking in specifically because it's an easy pair to transpose or confuse under exam pressure. Scope exhaustion is the most common practical failure: once every address in a DHCP pool is already leased out, the next client's DHCPDISCOVER goes unanswered and that client eventually self-assigns an APIPA address instead, a symptom this material's IPv4-addressing coverage already ties directly to a failed DHCP exchange. The DORA exchange, covered earlier in this material's IP-services coverage, can also fail partway through in a way that points to a specific culprit if the direction of the failing message is read correctly: a DHCPDECLINE is sent by the client, and only by the client, when it performs a final ARP check on the address it was just offered and finds that address already answering on the network; a DHCPNAK, by contrast, is sent by the server, and only by the server, to refuse a client's DHCPREQUEST outright — commonly because the client is asking to renew an address that no longer matches the subnet it's actually connected to. Mixing up which side sends which message is one of the most common ways this topic gets missed, since the two messages sound similar but originate from opposite ends of the exchange.

### Incorrect Gateway/IP/Subnet Mask & Duplicate IP Addresses

A handful of simple addressing mistakes account for a large share of otherwise-mysterious connectivity complaints. A wrong default gateway leaves a device able to reach everything on its own local subnet perfectly normally while being completely unable to reach anything beyond it, since the gateway is the only thing standing between a host and any network it isn't directly attached to. A wrong subnet mask is more subtle and more disruptive depending on which direction it's wrong in: a mask that's too short makes a device believe far more addresses are locally reachable than actually are, so it never asks the gateway to forward traffic that genuinely needed forwarding; a mask that's too long shrinks a device's own subnet in its own eyes, misclassifying some genuinely local addresses as remote and routing traffic through the gateway unnecessarily — RFC 950's original subnetting procedure is the foundational document formalizing exactly this kind of mask-driven local-versus-remote decision every IP host still makes today. A duplicate IP address produces one of the most disruptive and hardest-to-isolate symptoms in this entire category: two devices claiming the same address on the same network causes intermittent, connection-dropping behavior for both of them as ARP replies arrive inconsistently from whichever device answers first, and it's the exact condition a DHCP client's own ARP check (covered above) is designed to catch before it ever accepts an offered lease — which is why a duplicate address shows up far more often on statically configured hosts that never went through that same DHCP-side verification step.

## Performance Issues & Diagnostic Tooling

### Congestion, Bottlenecks & Bandwidth Constraints

Network congestion is fundamentally a supply-and-demand problem: more traffic wants to cross a given link or device than it has capacity to carry at that moment, and something has to give — packets queue up, and once a queue fills completely, packets start getting dropped outright. RFC 2914, the IETF's own statement of congestion-control principles, frames why this matters at a scale far beyond any one network: without endpoints and routers actually backing off in response to congestion signals, a busy link doesn't just slow down gracefully — throughput across the whole path can collapse well below what an uncongested link could have carried, since retransmissions from lost packets pile on top of the traffic that was already there. A bottleneck is simply the single slowest or most heavily loaded point along an end-to-end path — commonly a WAN link far narrower than the LAN on either side of it, an oversubscribed switch uplink, or an underpowered firewall doing more deep packet inspection than its hardware can keep up with — and because a path's real throughput can never exceed whatever that one narrowest point allows, finding it is usually a matter of checking interface utilization hop by hop until the one link or device running consistently hot turns up.

### Latency & Packet Loss

Latency is the time a packet takes to travel from source to destination, and it comes from several stacked sources that add together rather than one single cause: propagation delay from the raw physical distance a signal has to travel (the same reason this material's coverage of GEO satellite links calls out their noticeably higher round-trip time), queuing delay while a packet waits its turn on a congested link or device, and processing delay while a router or firewall actually inspects and forwards each packet. Packet loss is a related but distinct measurement — not how long a packet took, but whether it arrived at all — and it stems from causes that overlap with, but aren't identical to, latency's own: a full queue dropping packets outright once buffer space runs out, a physical-layer error corrupting a frame so badly it gets discarded before it ever reaches its destination, or a wireless link's signal degrading below the point where a frame can be decoded correctly at all. The two measurements matter differently depending on the traffic involved: a bulk file transfer tolerates high latency reasonably well since TCP simply retransmits and waits, but real-time voice and video traffic is far more sensitive to both figures at once, since a late-arriving voice packet is often no more useful than one that never arrived.

### Jitter: RFC 3550's Formal Definition

Jitter gets described casually as just variation in delay, but RFC 3550, the RTP specification, defines it far more precisely than that loose description suggests, and the exam-relevant version is the formal one. RFC 3550 defines interarrival jitter as the mean deviation — a smoothed absolute value — of the difference in relative transit time between a pair of consecutive packets, where relative transit time itself is the gap between a packet's own timestamp and the receiver's clock reading at the moment that packet actually arrives. Rather than averaging every packet gap seen across a whole session, RFC 3550 calculates this running estimate through exponential smoothing, updating the jitter value after every single packet using only the newest transit-time difference and the previous jitter estimate, weighted so that a burst of recent instability moves the number quickly while it settles back down just as quickly once conditions stabilize again. That running jitter estimate isn't just a debugging number computed and thrown away locally — RFC 3550 has a receiver report it back to the sender inside RTCP's own receiver report packets, which is what lets a sender, or anyone monitoring the session, see how unstable a call's packet spacing actually is without needing direct access to the receiving endpoint. High jitter is what makes a voice or video stream sound choppy even when every packet eventually arrives, since a receiver's playout buffer can only absorb so much variation in spacing before it runs dry or overflows.

### Wireless Interference, Coverage & Roaming Issues

Wireless performance complaints trace back to a smaller set of root causes than the range of symptoms might suggest. Interference comes in two flavors worth telling apart: co-channel interference from other Wi-Fi networks (or other access points in the same building) using the same or an overlapping channel, and non-Wi-Fi interference from other devices sharing the same unlicensed spectrum entirely, like a microwave oven or a cordless phone operating in the 2.4GHz band this material's frequency-band coverage already describes as tightly packed. Coverage problems are a more straightforward capacity-versus-distance issue: a signal simply weakens with distance and with each wall or obstruction it has to pass through, and a gap in coverage — a dead zone — is the direct result of an access point placement that leaves some physical area outside every radio's effective range. Roaming issues show up specifically in larger deployments running multiple access points sharing one ESSID, introduced earlier alongside BSSID and SSID: a client that hangs onto a weakening connection to its current access point for too long before switching to a stronger one nearby experiences a sticky-client problem, trading a small amount of the newer access point's better signal for the false comfort of staying associated with whichever radio it joined first — a tradeoff that gets worse the further a client physically wanders from that original access point.

### Core Diagnostic Commands: ping, traceroute, nslookup/dig

ping and traceroute both build on ICMP, defined in RFC 792 as the mechanism IP itself uses to report errors and diagnostic conditions back toward a sender, but the two tools use that same foundation to answer different questions. ping sends an ICMP echo request and waits for the matching echo reply, and per the Linux ping utility's own manual page that exchange is exactly what the tool is built to do — confirming a host is reachable at all and reporting round-trip time, without saying anything about the path a packet actually took to get there. traceroute answers that second question instead: per its own manual page, it works by launching probe packets with a deliberately small time-to-live value, starting at one and increasing it by one on each successive round, so that each probe expires exactly one hop further down the path and the router at that hop returns an ICMP message reporting the expiry — stitching those responses together traces the router-by-router path all the way to the destination, or reveals exactly which hop stopped responding if it never gets there. nslookup and dig round out the toolkit for the other half of a typical connectivity complaint — not whether a host is reachable, but whether a hostname resolves to the address it's supposed to in the first place — querying DNS directly and returning whatever record actually comes back, which isolates a DNS problem from a routing or reachability problem in a way ping and traceroute alone can't.

### Packet Capture, Discovery & Hardware Diagnostic Tools

Beyond the basic reachability commands, a deeper diagnostic toolkit rounds out troubleshooting for problems that ping and traceroute alone can't isolate. tcpdump, introduced earlier in this material's monitoring coverage, captures full packet contents rather than just confirming a path is reachable, which makes it the right choice once a problem needs to be understood at the level of exactly what a specific conversation contains — a malformed request, a TCP handshake that never completes, or a duplex-mismatch-driven pattern of retransmissions. Nmap, also introduced earlier in this material, serves troubleshooting in a narrower but still valuable way beyond its asset-discovery role: confirming whether a specific port on a specific host is actually listening at all settles, in one step, whether a reported connectivity failure is a network-path problem or simply a service that isn't running where it's expected to be. LLDP and CDP (Cisco's own proprietary equivalent) let a device announce its own identity, port, and capabilities directly to whatever's connected to it, which is what lets a technician confirm exactly which physical port on which switch a cable actually terminates on without needing to trust a possibly-stale wiring diagram. Cable testers and Wi-Fi analyzers — standard categories of handheld diagnostic hardware without a single citable standard defining them, the same way this domain's PoE and cabling coverage already treats several category names — round out the physical side of the toolkit: a cable tester verifies wire-map continuity and length on a suspect copper run directly, while a Wi-Fi analyzer surveys real-time channel utilization and signal strength across the wireless spectrum, catching problems no purely software-based tool sitting at either end of a link could ever see. Device show commands finally tie the whole toolkit together procedurally, since every technique above eventually gets cross-checked against a device's own reported interface counters, routing table, and configuration to confirm a diagnosis rather than just suspecting it.
