# Incident Response, On-Call & Postmortems

## On-Call Health, Escalation & Readiness

### Response Times Are Set by the SLO, Not by Habit

On-call engineers agree with the business owners of a service on a response-time window ahead of time, and that window isn't arbitrary — it traces back to the service's own availability target. Typical numbers the SRE book cites are close to five minutes of reaction time for a service that's user-facing or otherwise highly sensitive to time, and something more like thirty minutes for a system under looser demands. A service promising four nines of availability in a given quarter only has roughly thirteen minutes of allowed downtime for that entire quarter, which forces whoever is backing it to be reachable and acting within minutes; a looser SLO buys tens of minutes of slack instead. The workbook's own table of sample incidents makes the same point with concrete examples: a revenue-impacting network outage earns a five-minute response with the engineer expected to stay within arm's reach of a laptop, a stuck batch-processing job earns thirty minutes and room for a quick errand, and a failing backup on a pre-launch service doesn't page anyone at all — it becomes a ticket worked during business hours instead.

### The 'Two Incidents a Shift' Number Is a Staffing Heuristic, Not a Refusal Threshold

A frequently repeated on-call figure holds that a healthy rotation should see at most about two incidents per twelve-hour shift, but it's worth being precise about what that number is for. It comes from an observation that properly working an on-call incident all the way through — finding the root cause, remediating it, and following up afterward with a postmortem and any resulting bug fixes — takes roughly six hours on average, and two such incidents already fill the whole shift once sleep and a clean handoff are accounted for. Both books present this as a planning input for judging whether a rotation or a service is healthy, and as a bar any proposed new paging alert should be weighed against, not as a hard limit past which an on-call engineer may simply stop answering pages. The workbook's own 'team in overload' scenario makes that distinction explicit: a team budgeted for two incidents per shift but was regularly hitting five, and the fix wasn't to let pages go unanswered — it was treating the gap as evidence of operational overload and escalating for a dedicated fix-it project. The healthier signal both books point to is the shape of the whole distribution rather than the ceiling alone: the median incidents-per-shift figure should sit near zero, because a component that pages reliably once a day is telling you something is chronically broken.

### Minimum Team Size Falls Out of the 25% On-Call Ceiling

Google caps on-call and other operational work at no more than a quarter of an SRE's time, on top of a floor of at least half of every SRE's time reserved for engineering project work — and that constraint is what determines the smallest team that can sustain a rotation at all. Assuming a primary and a secondary are always staffed together and shifts last a week, the SRE book works out that a single-site team needs on the order of eight engineers before on-call becomes sustainable, while a team split across two sites can get by with a smaller headcount at each site, since spreading the rotation across time zones lets it 'follow the sun' and skip night shifts entirely — which matters, because working through the night carries measurable health costs of its own. The workbook revisits this arithmetic with a small refinement, arguing for padding the bare minimum by one extra engineer per site so the team can absorb someone stepping out of the rotation temporarily — for parental leave, illness, or similar circumstances — without pushing everyone else over the on-call ceiling. Either way, the underlying message is the same: on-call sustainability is a staffing math problem, not a matter of willpower, and understaffing a rotation eventually shows up as burnout, attrition, and postmortems that never get properly followed up.

### Primary and Secondary Aren't the Same Job

Most teams split on-call duty into a primary and a secondary role, but what the secondary actually does varies from team to team rather than following one fixed pattern. In the lightest arrangement, the secondary exists purely as a fallback for pages the primary misses, doing nothing at all unless that fall-through actually happens; in a busier arrangement, the secondary actively absorbs tickets and other non-paging production work so the primary can stay narrowly focused on pages alone. Teams too small to justify a dedicated secondary rotation sometimes solve the problem by pairing with a sister team and covering each other's fall-through pages, avoiding the overhead of a whole separate rotation just for backup coverage. What stays constant across all of these setups is that a single named primary engineer, not a group, answers each page — partly to sidestep the bystander effect where everyone assumes someone else has it, and partly to keep the interruption cost as small as possible — with anyone else, secondary included, pulled in only through an explicit escalation from that primary.

### Shift Length and the Case Against 24-Hour Rotations

The workbook is blunt about shift length: a rotation expecting at least a page a day should keep shifts to around twelve hours, because tired engineers make mistakes, and a full 24-hour stretch of on-call responsibility is not something most people can sustain without a real drop in judgment. A single-site team without the option of handing off across time zones can still hit a twelve-hour target by splitting a week between a day-shift engineer and a night-shift engineer rather than asking one person to carry the pager around the clock, and some teams shorten the rotation further, running something closer to three days on and four days off instead of a full week. Compensation for the out-of-hours burden this creates is treated as a given rather than an afterthought — Google offers time off in lieu or capped extra pay — both to recognize the cost fairly and to keep anyone from taking on more on-call than is healthy just to chase the additional pay.

### Underload Is a Real Failure Mode Too

It's tempting to frame on-call health purely around not overloading people, but the SRE book names the opposite extreme as its own hazard: an engineer who goes too long without carrying the pager for a genuinely active system loses the calibration that comes from regular exposure to production, and that gap in knowledge only becomes visible once a real incident forces them to act on assumptions that are already stale. The prescribed countermeasure is deliberate exposure — every engineer on a rotation should expect a couple of on-call turns each quarter even for a quiet service — paired with rehearsal that doesn't depend on a real page ever firing, such as 'Wheel of Misfortune' sessions that walk a group through a past incident, or Google's company-wide annual disaster-recovery testing that manufactures controlled emergencies on purpose. Both mechanisms exist to keep the muscle memory for incident response fresh between whatever real incidents happen to occur naturally, rather than letting it fade on a quiet system until the day it's genuinely needed.

## Incident Command: Roles & Coordination

### The SRE Book's Four Roles, Built on the Incident Command System

Google's approach to running a large incident is adapted from the Incident Command System, a coordination framework firefighters built decades ago and later exported into other kinds of emergency response. The SRE book's version of it names four distinct roles that can be handed off as an incident grows: an Incident Commander who tracks the overall state of the response and structures the task force around it; an Operations lead, the only person meant to actually change the system while the incident is live; a Communications lead, who acts as the response's public face and keeps stakeholders updated on a regular cadence; and a Planning lead, who takes on everything longer-horizon that would otherwise distract Ops — filing bugs, arranging staff handoffs, and tracking how far the system has drifted from its normal state so it can eventually be reverted. Any role nobody has explicitly taken defaults straight back to the Incident Commander, so nothing is ever left unowned, and the whole structure is meant to scale recursively: if one role gets overloaded, its holder can ask for more staff and spin off part of the work as its own sub-incident.

### War Rooms, Live Documents, and Explicit Handoffs

Beyond naming roles, running an incident well depends on a few concrete artifacts. Everyone involved needs to know where to reach the Incident Commander, whether that's a physical, designated command post or — just as common at a company spread across offices — an agreed-upon virtual one, such as a chat channel, which doubles as a searchable, timestamped log of what was tried and said and turns out to be valuable both mid-incident and later during the postmortem. The single most important artifact the commander maintains is a live, jointly editable incident document: a running summary, the current chain of command, a short set of exit criteria that define when the incident actually ends, an open list of outstanding bugs, and a timestamped log of events, kept messy if it has to be but always current. Handoffs at the end of a working day or across a time-zone boundary get deliberate ceremony rather than a quiet disappearance — the outgoing commander states plainly that command is transferring and stays on the call until the incoming commander explicitly confirms acceptance, and that change is broadcast to everyone else on the incident so nobody keeps taking direction from someone no longer in charge.

### The Workbook's Three Cs and Three Named Roles — A Real Difference From the SRE Book, Not a Typo

The workbook draws on the same Incident Command System heritage but frames things a little differently, and the difference is worth stating precisely rather than smoothing over. It organizes incident response around three goals it labels the 'three Cs' — coordinating the overall effort, communicating both inside the team and outward, and staying in control of how the response unfolds — and separately describes only three main named roles carrying out those goals inside Google's own framework: an Incident Commander, a Communications Lead, and an Operations Lead, with the latter two reporting up to the commander. There is no fourth, separately named Planning role among the workbook's core roles the way there is in the SRE book's chapter on managing incidents, and that's a genuine inconsistency between the two texts rather than something to quietly reconcile — the SRE book treats Planning as one of four coequal delegable roles, while the workbook's own account of how incidents run in practice describes a three-role core team built around the three Cs. Planning-style work like tracking bugs or arranging handoffs doesn't vanish under the workbook's framing, but it never gets elevated to a fourth headline role either, so a reader who only opened one of the two books would come away with a different count of what counts as 'the' core incident roles.

### Watching the Roles in Action Across Real Incidents

The workbook's case studies show these roles playing out with real people rather than staying abstract. In the Kubernetes cluster-creation outage, the on-call engineer who first noticed the failures, Zara, became Incident Commander by default simply because she was the one who declared the incident, about twenty-five minutes after the first page. Roughly two hours later, with a dozen people from multiple teams already crowded into the incident channel and still no confirmed root cause, a more senior engineer arriving for the day began organizing the response more formally, and rather than let a scheduled 10 a.m. handover between offices happen informally, command was deliberately passed to him; he then split out a dedicated Ops lead and Communications lead instead of continuing to let one person implicitly hold every role. A separate case, a datacenter power outage triggered by lightning, shows a cleaner run from the start: the on-call engineer with the best visibility into customer impact took command immediately, handed a set of well-understood recovery objectives to Operations, and kept a Communications lead busy full-time satisfying four distinct audiences at once — company leadership, internal teams waiting on a fix, general external customers, and specific customers who had already filed support tickets. PagerDuty's account of its own process adds a wrinkle neither Google case emphasizes as strongly: on incidents stretching past several hours, they deliberately rotate both the on-call engineers and the Incident Commander role itself roughly every four hours, explicitly to keep responders rested and bring fresh eyes to a stuck problem.

### Mitigate First, Understand Second

A recurring lesson across the workbook's case studies is that assigning roles only pays off in service of a specific priority order: assess the impact on users first, mitigate that impact next, and only once the bleeding has stopped turn to root-causing what actually happened — full understanding and a lasting fix can wait for the postmortem. The Kubernetes case study coins the term 'generic mitigation' for the class of action that follows from this ordering: a blunt, untargeted move like rolling back a suspicious recent release or rerouting traffic away from a suspect region, taken as soon as responders have a rough idea where a problem lives, well before they know exactly why it's happening. Generic mitigations aren't free — they're blunt instruments that can cause their own collateral disruption — but that same case study's retrospective shows that acting on a merely 'possible' cause identified mid-morning, rather than waiting roughly two more hours for the confirmed root cause, would have cut a six-hour-plus outage down considerably. The practical implication for how roles get designed is that Operations needs both the authority and the tooling to take that kind of blunt action fast, rather than waiting on a fully diagnosed root cause that a planning-style investigation might still be hours away from delivering.

### Practicing the Roles Before You Need Them

Both books treat incident-command competence as a trained skill rather than an instinct, and both push teams to rehearse the machinery before a real incident forces it on them. The SRE book's closing best-practice list encourages people to rotate which role they play each time the framework gets exercised, so the whole team builds familiarity with every seat instead of one or two people becoming the only ones who can run a large incident. PagerDuty describes a weekly exercise it calls Failure Friday, in which a nominated engineer — often someone training toward becoming a full Incident Commander — leads a real failure-injection drill using the same vocabulary and process a live incident would use, alongside separate time-boxed team exercises meant purely to build the habit of communicating quickly under pressure. Google's own version of the same idea, 'Wheel of Misfortune,' goes a step further by re-running a specific past incident with different engineers cast into its roles, sometimes with the original Incident Commander present to keep the replay honest, turning an old postmortem back into a live rehearsal instead of letting it sit as a document nobody revisits.

## Communicating Through a Crisis (and When to Declare One)

### The Living Incident Document as the Single Source of Truth

Once an incident is declared, the Incident Commander's most important ongoing job is a shared, live incident document that several people can edit at once rather than a static write-up finished after the fact. The book's own template shows the general shape this takes: a one-line summary kept current by the Communications lead, a status field, the location of the command post, the current chain of command so anyone can see at a glance who holds which role, a short set of exit criteria defining when the incident is genuinely over, an open list of filed bugs still outstanding, and a timestamped log that gets appended to as things happen instead of reconstructed afterward. The guidance explicitly allows this document to look messy — full of shorthand and half-finished thoughts — as long as it stays functional and current, with the most important information kept near the top so someone jumping in cold doesn't have to hunt for it. That same document then becomes the raw material for the eventual postmortem, which is part of why keeping it accurate in the moment matters well beyond the incident itself.

### Three Concrete Triggers for Declaring an Incident

Rather than leaving the call to individual judgment in the moment, the SRE book offers one team's concrete criteria for when a problem becomes a formally declared incident: does fixing it require pulling in a second team, whether customers can see the outage happening, or has it stayed unsolved after roughly an hour of focused analysis by whoever is already looking at it? If any one of those holds true, the guidance is to declare and stand up the incident-management structure rather than keep debugging solo. The reasoning behind erring toward declaring is asymmetric: declaring early and then finding a quick fix costs little beyond some extra documentation, while waiting hours into a spiraling problem before finally standing up formal coordination means losing exactly the structure — clear roles, a shared source of truth, deliberate communication — that would have kept the response from spiraling in the first place. The workbook echoes this with its own shorthand for the same idea, treating reluctance to declare early and often as one of the more common and more costly mistakes a team can make.

### What Not Declaring Actually Costs: The Google Home Case

The workbook's account of a Google Assistant quota bug shows what skipping an early declaration looks like in practice rather than in the abstract. Engineers noticed something was wrong days before the incident was ever formally declared, and in the interim, miscommunication between the client-side and server-side developers repeatedly sent the investigation down the wrong path, the true root cause stayed unidentified through several rounds of quota increases that only masked the symptom, and when the failure fully surfaced it did so over a weekend, requiring volunteer engineers to fix a problem the team hadn't treated as urgent enough to formally staff. The workbook is explicit about what an earlier declaration would have bought: it heads off exactly that kind of cross-team miscommunication, it tends to shorten how long root-causing and resolution take, and it gets the right people and the right external communications moving sooner. Contrast that with the Kubernetes cluster-creation incident, where the on-call engineer declared within about twenty-five minutes of the first page — the resulting incident still took over six hours to fully resolve, but every part of the response, from customer notification to eventually pulling in on-call engineers from six different teams, happened inside a structure that existed almost from the start.

### No Numbered Severity Scale — Google's Own Framework Is Qualitative

It's worth being precise about a gap in what these two books actually define: neither the SRE book's chapter on managing incidents nor the workbook's chapter on incident response lays out a numbered severity scale — there is no SEV0 through SEV5, no P0 through P4, built into Google's own incident-management framework. The declare-or-don't-declare decision described above is entirely qualitative, answered by asking whether a second team is needed, whether customers can see it, and whether it's gone unsolved for about an hour, not by looking a number up in a fixed table. The one place a numbered scheme does show up in these books is inside the workbook's on-call chapter, as part of Evernote's own account of how it runs on-call — Evernote sorts alerts into a homegrown three-tier paging priority and separately triages declared incidents into its own three-level severity scale, complete with its own escalation criteria for the top tier. That's presented explicitly as one company's practice, not as part of the shared vocabulary the rest of both books use for Google's own incidents, so treating numbered severity tiers as a universal SRE convention rather than as an Evernote-specific detail would be reading more into these two sources than they actually say.

### The Communications Lead's Job: Keeping Every Audience Informed Without Guessing Under Pressure

The person carrying the Communications-lead role during an incident is doing something distinct from debugging: their job is to keep every interested audience — company leadership, the rest of engineering, and, where relevant, external customers — informed on a predictable cadence, rather than leaving people to assume the worst from silence. The power-outage case study is a useful illustration of just how many distinct audiences that can mean at once: leaders wanting reassurance the problem was under control, internal teams needing a timeline for when their storage would return, external customers who needed proactive notice about disk issues in their region, and specific customers who had already filed support tickets and needed individually tailored answers. Because drafting public-facing statements from scratch during a live incident is exactly the kind of task nobody does well under pressure, the recommended preparation is to settle on a communication channel well in advance — a phone bridge, a chat channel, whatever the team already knows how to use — and to have a couple of ready-to-adapt announcement templates and a prepared contact list sitting ready before they're ever needed, rather than improvised for the first time mid-outage.

## Blameless Postmortems & Organizational Learning

### Blameless Doesn't Mean No Accountability — It Means the Blame Isn't Aimed at People

The core tenet of Google's postmortem culture is that a postmortem should assume everyone involved acted in good faith and did their best with the information available at the time, and that assumption of good intent is what gives people the confidence to describe honestly what happened instead of hiding or minimizing it. But blameless is easy to misread as 'no consequences,' and the books are careful to head that off: a genuinely blameless postmortem still has to produce concrete, owned follow-up actions, because a written record of what went wrong that changes nothing is functionally the same as never having investigated at all. Google's own VP for operations is quoted making that expectation blunt — every postmortem following a user-affecting outage is expected to carry at least one high-priority tracking bug, with exceptions personally reviewed by him and granted only rarely. So the accountability in a blameless culture isn't gone, it's relocated: it moves off individual people and onto named owners of specific, trackable fixes to the system and its processes.

### Why 'Train Humans Not to Run Unsafe Commands' Is a Weak Action Item

The workbook's paired weak and strong postmortems over the same real incident make the accountability-without-blame distinction concrete rather than theoretical. In the weaker version, the root-cause section names a single engineer as having disregarded the automation setup and run a workflow manually, and one resulting action item is essentially to train people not to repeat that — both moves treat the incident as a story about one person's mistake rather than about a system that let that mistake cause damage at a global scale. The rewritten version tells a completely different story from the same underlying facts: it traces the failure to a specific input-validation gap in an admin server, a missing safety check in a separate machine-management system, and a workflow that wasn't actually safe to rerun the way the team had assumed, and its action items go after exactly those gaps — add the missing validation, add the safety check, rate-limit the workflow — each with a named owner and a tracking bug. The broader principle drawn out of the contrast is that changing automation and system design is a far more reliable lever than trying to change how careful a person is in the future, since a validation check protects against a mistake every single time, while training only helps until someone forgets or a new person joins who never received it.

### When a Postmortem Is Required

Rather than leaving the decision to whoever happens to be closing out an incident, the SRE book lists concrete triggers that make writing a postmortem effectively mandatory: user-visible downtime or degradation beyond an agreed threshold, any data loss, an on-call engineer having to intervene directly in production such as a rollback or a traffic reroute, a resolution time that exceeded some agreed bar, or a monitoring failure serious enough that the incident was found manually rather than through alerting. Defining these triggers ahead of time, before any specific incident is on the table, is what keeps the decision from becoming political in the moment — nobody has to argue in real time about whether a given outage was 'bad enough' to warrant the write-up. On top of that objective list, any stakeholder can request a postmortem for an event that doesn't technically trip one of the defined triggers but still seems worth understanding, which keeps the criteria from acting as a ceiling as well as a floor.

### Action Items Need an Owner, a Bug, a Priority, and a Way to Tell When They're Done

The single most consistent difference the workbook draws out between weak and strong postmortems is how rigorously the action items get specified. A weak action item is phrased as an ambiguous verb with no measurable end state, is assigned the same priority as everything else on the list, and often has no tracking bug at all — a combination that all but guarantees it never actually gets done. A strong action item reads more like a shippable engineering task: it names exactly one person as the point of contact, even if others collaborate on it, links to a bug in the team's normal tracking system, carries an explicit priority so readers know what to tackle first, and is worded so that anyone could later check whether it was actually completed, such as adding an alert that fires above a specific numeric threshold rather than vaguely promising 'better alerting.' Grouping related action items under shared themes — prevention, emergency response, monitoring, and so on — is a lighter-weight technique the workbook recommends for incidents large enough to generate dozens of items at once, purely so reviewers can assign ownership and priority theme by theme instead of working through one undifferentiated list.

### Getting a Postmortem Reviewed, Shared, and Actually Read

A postmortem that only the involved team ever reads has delivered a fraction of its possible value, so both books push toward wide, structured sharing rather than narrow, informal circulation. The recommended workflow has an initial draft reviewed by a handful of senior engineers against a short checklist — was the incident data properly captured, is the impact assessment complete, does the root-cause analysis actually go deep enough, is the action plan well scoped and appropriately prioritized — before the document is published broadly, ideally to the whole engineering organization rather than just the owning team, since a postmortem's value scales with how many people can learn from it. Beyond that formal review, the books describe lighter cultural rituals that keep postmortems alive well past their publication date: a periodic newsletter pick highlighting a well-written example, informal reading clubs where a team walks through an old incident together, and 'Wheel of Misfortune' sessions where newer engineers reenact a past incident's roles, sometimes with the original Incident Commander in the room. On the incentive side, the workbook is explicit that rewarding people only for writing postmortems, and not for actually closing out the action items afterward, quietly produces a pile of well-written documents that nothing ever changed because of.

### What a Complete Postmortem Actually Looks Like

Google's own worked example — a fictionalized search-outage postmortem — shows the shape a complete document tends to take. Up top sits a compact executive summary: what broke, how badly, the root cause and the specific trigger that set it off, how it was resolved, and how it was first detected. Below that comes a table of action items, each tagged by type — whether it mitigates the current class of problem, prevents it outright, or is more of a process fix — alongside an owner and a bug link, with several already marked complete by the time the postmortem is published rather than left as open promises. A 'lessons learned' section splits cleanly into what went well, what went wrong, and, notably, a separate section for near misses that didn't cause real damage this time but easily could have — the kind of detail that's easy to skip and genuinely useful to capture anyway. Finally, a minute-by-minute timeline reconstructs the whole incident from first anomaly to formal close, which is exactly the sort of detail that only survives if it's carried over from a live incident document kept current in the moment rather than reconstructed from memory afterward.
